The shift

You are not the craftsperson any more.
You run the factory.

Old shape

  • One person who knows the language, the files, and where everything lives.
  • That knowledge sits in one head.
  • How fast you ship depends on how fast you type.

Factory shape

  • You specify. Agents build. Machines check.
  • The knowledge sits in the repository.
  • How fast you ship depends on how clearly you say what you want.

The bottleneck is no longer typing. It is deciding what done means.

4 min

Ask who has waited on the one person who knew how the system worked. That person is the bottleneck we are replacing.

The factory comparison is literal, not decoration. A factory has stations with defined outputs, and an inspector between the line and the customer. Same shape here, except agents run most of the stations.

The line

Six stations, one loop

Every station produces something you can point at. Nothing moves on until that thing exists.

4 min

Walk the six boxes with your hand. The names matter less than the outputs: a brief document, a transcript of decisions, a one page spec, numbered issues, commits, a merge.

The stations numbered in accent colour need a human. The rest can run with an agent unsupervised, at least at first.

Point at the return arrow. Milestone one ships, and the machine that built it is still standing for milestone two.

Station 01The brief

Start with everything you know

Facts first, in one dump. The agent cannot invent your constraints, so the only mistake here is leaving one out.

  • What it does: one sentence, in plain words.
  • Who uses it: what device, what room.
  • Must and must not: the rules that are not negotiable.
  • Constraints: systems it has to talk to, budget, deadline, policy.
  • Taste: two things you like, two you hate, with names.

Rambling is fine. The interview tightens it. A thin brief is the one thing no station downstream can fix.

WHAT YOU TYPE

I want to build <one sentence>.

Here is everything I know:
- who uses it:
- where they use it:
- it must:
- it must never:
- it has to work with:
- done means:
- I hate when software:

Do not build anything yet.
Read this, then interview me
until you can write the spec.

6 min

Do a live example with something from the room. Ask for an idea and type the brief in front of them, badly at first. The mess is the lesson: a brief does not have to be well written, it has to be complete.

Emphasise the last line. Without it, most agents start generating a project skeleton before the interview ever happens, and you get code instead of answers.

Facts beat adjectives. "Loads in under a second on the office wifi" is a fact. "Fast and modern" is a wish.

Station 02The grilling

It interviews you, adversarially

The agent's job here is to find the vague and contradictory parts of the idea while they are still cheap to fix.

  • Nothing gets built while the questions are open.
  • Every answer becomes a line in the spec, or an explicit "out of scope".
  • Vague answers get pushed until they are testable.
  • "I don't know" is an answer. Ask it to decide and tell you why.
  • It may argue with you. That is the job.
  • You end it, not the agent. Stop when answers stop changing.

10 min

Adversarial means it attacks the idea, not you. Expect a wall of questions, and answer in shorthand because the agent does the writing.

Demo it if you can. Take the brief from the last slide and ask for a grilling, and let the room watch twenty questions go past. Then point at the two that surfaced something you had not thought about. That moment sells the workflow better than any slide.

Watch for the failure: an agent that starts building anyway. The last line of the brief prevents it.

Nobody here is being asked for technical answers. "Should it email me or show a badge?" is a question about their own job, and it is exactly what this station produces.

Station 03The spec

A spec is a contract you can test

One page. If it needs more, the idea is not settled. Go back to the grilling.

It contains

  • The promise: what it does, in the user's words.
  • Behaviours: numbered, each one checkable.
  • Out of scope: what it deliberately will not do.
  • Open questions: with a name against each one.

A behaviour reads like this

3. Given a signed-in user,
   when they enter the wrong password,
   then the screen says
   "That password is wrong"
   and the field is cleared.

# checkable by a person
# and by a machine

Wrong at the spec: a conversation.
Wrong after the build: the build.

6 min

This is the hinge. Everything downstream is mechanical, and this is the last station where a decision is cheap.

Read a spec aloud in the room. Circle any sentence that cannot become a yes or no check, and that circle is the next grilling round.

Out of scope is not a rejection. It is a promise you are keeping, and writing it down stops the ask in week three.

It lives in the repository as SPEC.md, next to the code and versioned like everything else.

Station 04Tickets

One ticket, one check, one commit

The spec gets cut into slices small enough to finish, check, and drop if the slice turns out wrong.

Too big, never finishes

"Build the login system."

No end and no check: weeks of agent work with nothing to hand over.

Right size, finishes today

"A wrong password shows an error and clears the field."

One behaviour, one check, one commit, one closed ticket.

  • If you cannot write the check, the ticket is too vague, not too small.
  • Slice by behaviour, not by layer of the system.
  • Foundations first: the thing that stores data before the thing that shows it.
  • A grilling ticket is real work. A design session is a deliverable.

7 min

Use the service desk comparison, because this room already thinks in tickets. An incident has a symptom, an owner and a close condition. Same shape.

The test of a good ticket: could somebody who missed this meeting pick it up and know when it is finished? If not, split it again.

One commit is a rule of thumb. The rule that matters is one checkable behaviour.

Say the grilling ticket out loud. In a real project of mine, three features each got a ticket that was only "run the design session". Each one closed with a document instead of code, and that was correct.

Station 05Red / green

Write the check before the code

The check has to fail first. A test that has never failed has never tested anything.

  • Red proves the check can fail. Without that, a green result means nothing.
  • Green proves the change did what the ticket asked.
  • Commit at green, so every commit is a working state.
  • The agent runs the loop. You read the result.

8 min

The comparison that lands is a checklist: the agent may not tick a box it has not first watched fail. Otherwise done is an opinion.

This station is what protects you from confident nonsense. An agent can write a check that always passes, and red first catches it.

Do not teach them to write tests. Teach them to ask for red proof: show me the check failing before you fix it. That one sentence is worth the whole station.

Station 06Review & merge

The agent's work is a proposal, not a delivery

Done is a claim. A merge is evidence.

Ask these four

  • What did it change, in plain language?
  • What did it leave out that the ticket asked for?
  • Where is the check that failed first?
  • What did this break that used to work?

Then a second agent reads it

  • A fresh agent reviews the change with no memory of writing it.
  • It hunts for gaps, not style.
  • You read both lists and decide.

Merge means this is now the machine. Everything after it is maintenance of your own decision.

6 min

Non-programmers can review. They cannot read the code, but they can read the summary, the ticket and the evidence, and they can ask what was left out. That question finds most of the problems.

The reviewer agent is not ceremony. The author is biased toward its own work, and a second one is not.

The logbook

Git is the factory's memory

You will not type git commands; the agent does. You need the nouns, because every conversation about the work uses them.

commitA snapshot of everything, with a note saying why it changed.
branchA parallel copy of the project for one change, so main keeps working.
pull request"Here is my change, please check it." A conversation with a diff attached.
mergeThe change joins main. From here it is everyone's problem.
historyEvery snapshot, who made it, when, and why. Nobody has to remember.

The rule that matters: commit small, and make the message say why. "Fix bug" is worthless in six months. "Reject the wrong password, because the field kept the old value" is a paragraph of memory.

8 min

Do not open a terminal. Use the diagram. If they remember one thing, it is that main is what is live and a branch is somewhere safe to try.

Commit messages are the cheapest habit here, and the one they can enforce from day one: make the message say why.

Reassure them. Agents run the commands, and mistakes are recoverable because git keeps every snapshot, including the bad ones.

The plan

Milestones are the plan.
Issues are the work.

  • Issue: a numbered ticket with the ask, the conversation, and the close condition.
  • Milestone: a group of issues that ship together, like a release.
  • Labels: bug, spec, blocked. Cheap, and they end arguments.
  • Chat is not a plan. If it is not an issue, nobody is tracking it.

7 min

This is where the factory becomes visible to management. The board is the line's state, and the milestone is the shipment date.

A real one from this term: a milestone of eleven issues, foundation first, then the visible features, then the documentation. All eleven closed and deployed.

Enforce the close condition. An issue closes with a commit reference, not with a feeling, or the board lies within a week.

The inspector

A robot checks every change

CI is a fresh machine that builds the project and runs every check, every time. Nobody argues with the robot.

  • CI runs on every push and every pull request, not on your laptop.
  • Green means allowed to merge. Red means blocked.
  • CD takes green main and deploys it, the same way every time.
  • It replaces "it worked on my machine" with evidence.

8 min

Ask how often something worked for them and then failed for the person next to them. That gap is what CI closes, because a clean room is not your desk.

Two rules. Nothing merges red, and the deploy path is identical every time. Hand made deploy steps are where factories quietly stop shipping.

If they take one operational habit away, make it this: a red build is the highest priority ticket in the building.

The floor

Everything lives in the repository

The repository holds more than code: the decisions, the checks, and the code itself.

project/
├── BRIEF.md        what you asked for
├── SPEC.md         what it will do
├── docs/adr/      why decisions were made
├── README.md      how to run it
├── .github/       the robot's instructions
├── tests/         the checks
└── src/           the code
issues + milestones live beside this, numbered
  • A decision with no recorded reason gets argued again every month.
  • The next agent reads it cold, with no memory of your meeting.
  • Code without its brief, spec and checks is just a folder of files.

Write for the next agent. It is a new colleague every morning: fast, literal, and ignorant of everything you said out loud.

5 min

This is the habit that decides whether the workflow survives a real week. Everything else can be improvised. This cannot.

Name the ADR folder. Architecture decision record sounds grand, but it is one page saying what we chose, what we rejected and why. Six months later it is the only thing that ends the same argument.

The roles

What the agent does, and what you do

The agentYou
Asks the grilling questionsAnswers them, and decides the trade-offs
Writes the spec draft, the tickets, the codeSigns the spec, cuts scope, says no
Runs the red/green loopAsks for the failing check as proof
Commits, pushes, reads CI outputReviews the summary and the evidence
Never gets tired, never gets boredNotices when the thing being built is wrong

Your hours go to stations 1, 2, 3 and 6.
The agent's hours go to 4 and 5.

The harness is a preference, not the method. opencode is what I use; any agent that can read a repository, run commands and be interrupted will do. The order of the stations is the part that cannot vary.

4 min

This slide stops both failures at once: expecting the agent to know what you want, and expecting yourself to review every line of code.

Harness choice is free. The stations are the method, the tool is a preference, and the order is what holds it together.

What goes wrong

Five ways factories stall

SymptomCause and fix
Confident work on the wrong thingNo spec, no sign-off. Gate at station 3, every time.
A ticket that never endsToo big. Slice by behaviour until it fits a day.
All checks pass, product brokenChecks written after the code. Write them red first.
Works for the agent, not for usersNo clean room. CI on every push, not on one laptop.
Nothing ever shipsMain is never green, or there is no deploy path. Deploy weekly, even when it is small.

The one that hides best: an agent that says done and means "I wrote something". Ask for evidence, not the claim. Evidence is a check that failed and then passed, a green pipeline, and a running thing you can open.

5 min

Ask which of the five they recognise from their own IT work. Usually all five, which is the point: agents amplify an undisciplined process instead of fixing it.

The third row deserves the emphasis. It is the failure that looks like success, and the red step is the only cure.

Exercise

Your first lap

Pick something you can show a colleague in one week. That size is the point: it finishes.

  1. Write the brief. Twenty minutes, no editing.
  2. Ask for the grilling. Answer everything.
  3. Read the spec out loud. Sign it.
  4. Cut tickets. Take the smallest one that shows something.
  1. Red, then green. Ask to see the red.
  2. Push. Watch CI go green.
  3. Merge, deploy, open it in a browser.
  4. Show one person. Then take the next ticket.

10 min, or the rest of the session, live

Best use of this slide: do it with the room. Ask for one volunteer's idea, write the brief together, run the grilling live and stop at the spec. They will have watched the front half of the factory in twenty minutes.

A worked example to point at: the club's own MUD. One grilled spec (SPEC.md), milestone M1, and 28 tickets that each carry a red step and a green step. That is this slide, already done.

If they do it alone afterwards: one week, one visible thing, deployed somewhere you can open it.
